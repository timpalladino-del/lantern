# Why Lantern: the case for keeping track

*Draft argument for the website, the App Store description and the press kit. Figures are from NOAA, FEMA, state
agencies and news reporting; where sources disagree, the lower or most widely reported figure is used.*

## The decade we just lived through

Nobody has to imagine the emergency anymore. In the last ten years it has arrived, somewhere in America, roughly
every two weeks.

- **2017.** Harvey put Houston under water for days and caused well over $100 billion in damage. Irma emptied
  Florida's shelves and gas stations before it made landfall. Maria took power from over 90 percent of Puerto Rico,
  some of it for nearly a year. The costliest disaster year on record, at $395 billion.
- **2018.** The Camp Fire erased Paradise, California, and killed 85 people, many in their cars trying to leave.
  Michael hit the Panhandle as a Category 5. Anchorage shook with a magnitude 7.1 earthquake.
- **2019.** Spring floods across Nebraska, Iowa and Missouri. Ridgecrest, California, took two major quakes in two
  days. Tornadoes hit Dayton, Ohio, on Memorial Day night and the power stayed off for days.
- **2020.** For the first time in history, every state had a federal major disaster declaration at once, for
  COVID-19. Then a derecho flattened Iowa in forty minutes, the costliest thunderstorm in U.S. history, and a
  record wildfire season burned through Oregon, Washington and California. Six hurricanes made U.S. landfall.
- **2021.** Winter Storm Uri froze Texas. Seventy percent of the state lost power, pipes burst in millions of homes,
  and at least 246 people died, most of them in the cold and the dark. Ida knocked out power to over a million
  homes in Louisiana, then drowned people in basement apartments in New York. A December tornado ran 165 miles
  through Kentucky at night and killed dozens in Mayfield.
- **2022.** Ian hit Fort Myers and killed around 150 people, most by storm surge. Eastern Kentucky flooded in July
  and 45 people died. Winter Storm Elliott buried Buffalo at Christmas and 47 people died in Erie County, many in
  their homes or cars.
- **2023.** Lahaina, Hawaii, burned in an afternoon: 102 people dead, a town gone. Vermont flooded. Rolling Fork,
  Mississippi, was torn apart by a tornado at night. NOAA counted 28 billion-dollar disasters, the most ever.
- **2024.** Helene came ashore in Florida and did its worst in the mountains of North Carolina, a place nobody
  thought of as hurricane country. At least 232 people died across six states and 4.7 million customers lost
  power. Two weeks later Milton crossed Florida and cut power to over 3 million. Twenty-seven billion-dollar
  disasters in one year.
- **2025.** Los Angeles burned in January: 31 dead, more than 16,000 homes and buildings destroyed, in neighborhoods
  with hydrants and fire stations. In July the Guadalupe River rose over twenty feet in an hour in the Texas Hill
  Country and 135 people died, dozens of them children at summer camp. For the first time in 45 years, NOAA
  stopped counting billion-dollar disasters in May; the independent count reached 23 by year end.
- **2026.** A January winter storm from Texas to the Northeast left more than a million without power and 181 people
  dead, and we are only in November.

Add it up. Over the last ten years the U.S. has had about 190 separate billion-dollar disasters, more than 6,300
deaths from them, and roughly $1.4 trillion in damage. FEMA now issues around 60 to 90 major disaster declarations
a year, nearly double the pace of the 1990s. In 2024 insured losses topped $100 billion for the fifth year in a
row.

## What those events had in common

Look past the headlines and the same things happen every time:

1. **The power goes out, and stays out.** Not for an hour. For three days in Houston, five in Louisiana, ten in
   western North Carolina, weeks in Puerto Rico. The refrigerator becomes a cooler on the first day and a problem
   on the second.
2. **The stores close or empty.** Before Irma, bottled water sold out across Florida in 48 hours. After Helene,
   Asheville had no running water for seven weeks. You cannot shop your way out once it has started.
3. **Help takes days.** FEMA's own guidance is to be ready to look after yourself for at least 72 hours, and the
   Red Cross says two weeks if you are staying home. Both numbers exist because that is how long it takes for help
   to reach a neighborhood after roads, bridges and cell towers are gone.
4. **The people who died were mostly at home.** Uri, Elliott, Ida, the Hill Country. Not hikers or thrill-seekers.
   Families in houses, in their own beds, in their own cars. In the cold, in the water, running a generator in the
   garage to keep the kids warm.
5. **Almost nobody was ready.** Surveys keep finding the same thing: only about 5 percent of American homes have a
   fully stocked kit, one in five has nothing at all, and fewer than one in five could last a month on what is in
   the house. Not because people don't care. Because the storm feels far away until it isn't.

## The real problem is not buying. It's keeping.

Here is what the research on preppers, non-preppers and everyone in between actually shows. People do buy.
Thirty percent of American adults spent money on preparedness last year, about $11 billion. A storm hits the news,
a case of water and some batteries go in the cart, a bucket of emergency meals goes in the basement. And then
nothing. The water sits past the point the CDC says to rotate it. The batteries corrode in the flashlight. The
medication runs out on day four because nobody counted. The generator that was bought after the last outage
won't start, because it hasn't been run in two years. The cans in the back of the closet turn out to be from
2019.

The failure is never knowledge. Everyone knows what a kit should have; Ready.gov has had the list online for
twenty years. The failure is memory. A household is not a warehouse, and nobody has a system for the twenty
things in six places that quietly expire. Spreadsheets get built and abandoned within weeks. Index cards get
lost. The result is that when the lights go out, the family that spent $500 getting ready discovers they are
ready for about a day and a half.

## What Lantern does about it

Lantern is the system. It is the part everyone skips.

- It knows what you have, where it is and when it stops being good, because you scanned it once instead of
  writing it down.
- It tells you what to use this week, so the extra food becomes dinner instead of waste, and the shelf stays
  fresh without you thinking about it.
- It counts the people in your house, their ages, their medications, their pets, and tells you in days, not
  guesses, how long you are covered. Not "a readiness score." Days.
- It remembers to run the generator, test the alarms, check the extinguisher and top up the power station, and
  it tells you when you have gone too long.
- It works with no signal and no power, because it never needed a server in the first place.

None of this requires a bunker or a plan for the end of the world. It is the same impulse that keeps a spare tire
in the trunk and a smoke alarm in the hall. The storms are not hypothetical. The one in your region has a season,
and it is on the calendar.

Ready for when you need it most. That is the whole pitch.
